4-axis motorized camera platform

Stepper motor control and electronics for a cell-sample imaging rig used during electrochemical experiments. The physical structure was designed by the supervising researcher. I built the electronics, configured the firmware, debugged a coil-wiring problem that masqueraded as a firmware fault, and wrote the operating manual for handoff.

The use case

The Baker Group runs electrochemical experiments where multiple cell samples need to be imaged at controlled positions over the course of an experiment that can run for hours. The supervising researcher wanted a rig that could move a camera through a defined position pattern repeatably so imaging conditions stayed constant across runs and across samples. He designed the physical structure; he asked me to make the motion work.

What I built

Four axes of stepper motion driven by a BigTreeTech SKR Mini E3 v2.0 motor controller running Marlin firmware over USB serial. Four NEMA17 stepper motors on the four axes, with a wiring harness tying the controller, motors, limit switches, and power supply together inside the existing structure. I configured Marlin’s motion parameters — steps per millimeter, acceleration limits, microstepping, per-axis current via the onboard TMC2209 drivers — for the specific kinematics of the rig.

The BTT SKR Mini E3 plus Marlin is a 3D printer stack, not an obvious fit for a non-printer lab instrument. The reason it works: it’s a very mature stack for synchronized multi-axis stepper control with TMC2209 driver feedback over UART, and the G-code interface maps cleanly to “move axis N to position X at speed Y,” which is exactly what the researcher needed to script the imaging routine. Building a custom controller from scratch would have taken weeks. This stack got it working in days.

The wiring problem

The first version of the system was unstable in a way that took a while to localize. Motors would shake during moves rather than step cleanly, and after a move the driver settings would revert to the EEPROM defaults instead of holding the values I had flashed in. Both symptoms pointed at firmware. I reflashed Marlin multiple times, verified the configuration, watched the settings reload, watched them revert on the next move.

Running the TMC2209 driver status report (M122) confirmed what I was seeing: the driver UART connection was dropping after each move, and the settings I had flashed in were getting replaced by EEPROM defaults each time the driver re-initialized. So the problem wasn’t the EEPROM or the firmware. Something was forcing the driver to re-initialize on every move.

That pointed to hardware. I went back to the SKR Mini’s manual, looked at the motor connector pinout on the board, and ran a multimeter across the stepper motor pins to map the coil pairs. The cables didn’t match the board. The board labels its motor connector pins 2B, 1B, 1A, 2A. The cables were routing one wire of each motor coil to an inner pin and the other to an outer pin, splitting both coils across both driver pairs. The drivers can’t drive a split coil. They fault on the first move, the UART drops, and the driver re-initializes from EEPROM defaults on the next command.

The lab had a mix of stepper motors and cables collected over time, some of which had different coil-connector pinouts. Each motor had to be checked against its cable and the board before commissioning. The fix was straightforward once the cause was clear: identify each motor’s coil pairs with a multimeter, re-pin the connectors so each coil terminates on the correct pair, and re-verify with M122. The diagnostic and verification procedure is the longest section of the operating manual.

Handoff

Once the platform was running reliably, I wrote an operating manual covering Pronterface setup, the pre-session checklist, the G-code command set the researcher would actually use, the software travel limits that silently stop motion past 235mm, the full M122 driver status reference, an error-signature table mapping the common fault flags to root cause and recovery action, the coil-wiring verification procedure, and the firmware calibration order for when the mechanical setup changes.

The researcher uses the rig directly in experiments. I’m not in the loop on runtime. The manual is the contract between the hardware as I built it and the researcher running experiments on it.

Role

The physical structure of the platform was designed by the supervising researcher, not by me. My scope was the controller selection, the wiring harness, the firmware configuration, the debug, and the documentation.
